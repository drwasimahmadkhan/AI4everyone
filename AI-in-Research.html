01 · Understand AI

What is AI, and how does it actually work?

It has read a lot

Trained on huge text

Tools like ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are called Large Language Models (LLMs). They have been trained on a massive amount of text — books, articles, websites and code — so they have "seen" how humans explain almost every subject.

It predicts words

It does not "look up" answers

When you ask a question, the AI does not search a database of facts. It predicts the most likely next words, one by one, based on patterns it learned. That is why its answers are so fluent and natural.

The consequence

Fluent — but sometimes wrong

Because it predicts instead of retrieving, it is excellent at explaining, summarising, translating and drafting — but it can also confidently write things that are not true. This is called hallucination.

Remember this one idea: AI produces likely text, not verified truth.Fluent ≠ correct
02 · Understand AI

The famous AI assistants you should know

OpenAI

ChatGPT

The most popular all-rounder. Very good for explanations, brainstorming ideas, improving writing, and general research questions. Free version available; the paid version is stronger.

Anthropic

Claude

Excellent with long documents. You can give it a full research paper or a whole thesis chapter and discuss it in detail. Known for careful, well-structured answers.

Google

Gemini

Google's AI assistant. Works well with images and videos, connects with Google apps, and powers NotebookLM — a special research tool we will see later.

Microsoft

Copilot

Built into Word, Excel, PowerPoint and Windows. Useful when you want AI help directly inside the documents and spreadsheets you already work in.

AI + Search

Perplexity

An AI-powered search engine. It answers your question and shows clickable sources for every claim — very useful when you need answers you can check.

Free option

DeepSeek

A free chatbot that became famous for strong reasoning and coding ability. A good option when you need a capable AI without paying for a subscription.

You do not need all of them — pick one main assistant and learn it well.One tool used well beats five tools used poorly
03 · Strengths

What AI can do for you as a researcher

Learn

A patient teacher

It can explain any difficult concept in simple words, give examples from your own field, and answer your questions again and again — without ever getting tired or judging you.

Save time

A fast assistant

It can summarise long documents, organise messy notes, translate text between languages, and prepare first drafts in seconds — work that normally takes hours.

Analyse

A data & code helper

It can suggest the right statistical test, write analysis code for SPSS, Python, R or Excel, and explain every step of the analysis in plain language.

Improve

An honest critic

It can review your proposal or draft, point out weak arguments and missing pieces, and suggest improvements — before a real supervisor or reviewer sees it.

In every role, AI suggests — and you decide.AI proposes · the researcher disposes
04 · Limits

Where AI goes wrong — be careful

×

It invents references

Ask it for "10 papers on your topic" and it may confidently list papers, authors and journals that do not exist. Every reference must be checked in Google Scholar before you use it.

×

It is not a live database

A basic chatbot does not search real journals or the internet — and its knowledge stops at a cut-off date. For real papers, use the research tools we will see shortly.

×

Confidence ≠ correctness

AI sounds equally sure when it is right and when it is wrong. Its confident tone is a writing style, not a signal of truth — never judge an answer by how certain it sounds.

×

It takes no responsibility

If a mistake reaches your thesis or paper, the responsibility is yours alone. "The AI told me so" is not a defence in front of a supervisor, examiner or journal.

None of these problems stops you from using AI — they tell you HOW to use it.Rule №1: verify everything before it enters your research
05 · Prompting

How to talk to AI: the 5-part prompt recipe

1 · Role — Tell the AI who it should be: "You are an experienced research supervisor…"

2 · Context — Tell it who you are and what you are doing: "I am a Master's student in education, new to research…"

3 · Task — Say exactly what you want: explain, compare, list, summarise, review, suggest.

4 · Format — Say how you want the answer: a table, 5 bullet points, simple words, with examples.

5 · Follow up — Never stop at the first answer. Say "explain it more simply", "give another example", or "what is wrong with this answer?"

Weak prompt

"Tell me about research methodology."

Result: a long, generic essay that helps nobody.

Strong prompt

"You are a research supervisor. I am a Master's student in education, completely new to research. Explain the difference between qualitative and quantitative research in simple words, in a short table, with one example of each from education."

Result: a focused, usable answer at your level.

The quality of the answer depends on the quality of the question.Good prompt in → good answer out

07 · Steps 1–2 · Topic & learning

Learn any new topic with AI

Start simple. Ask AI to explain the concept as if you are completely new to it. If the answer is still difficult, say "explain it even more simply."

Ask for examples. An example from your own field (education, business, health…) makes an abstract idea concrete and memorable.

Let it quiz you. Ask the AI to test you with questions. Answering them shows you what you actually understood.

The real test: close the chat and explain the concept in your own words. If you cannot, you have read an answer — but not learned it yet.

Copy this prompt — change the [topic]
Explain [multiple regression] in simple words,
as if I am completely new to statistics.

Then:
1. Give one example of it from
   [education research].
2. List the 3 most common mistakes
   beginners make with it.
3. Ask me 3 questions to test my
   understanding — and wait for my answers.
Works for any concept: sampling, validity, ANOVA, thematic analysis, literature review…Learn WITH AI — don't just read FROM it
08 · Step 3 · Your question

From a broad topic to a focused research question

Too broad
"Social media and students."
You cannot study everything about this — who? what effect? measured how? where?

Focused
"How does daily TikTok use affect the sleep quality of university students in Lahore?"
Now it is clear who is studied, what is measured, and where — this can actually be researched.

AI's job here: generate many possible questions quickly, so you can compare them and pick the one that is interesting AND realistic for your time and resources.

Copy this prompt — change the [topic]
My broad topic is
[social media and university students].

1. Suggest 6 focused research questions
   from this topic.
2. For each question, tell me:
   • who will be studied,
   • what exactly will be measured,
   • whether it is realistic for a
     6-month student project.
3. Tell me which ONE question you would
   choose, and why.
AI suggests the options — but YOU choose the question, because you will live with it.A clear question is half the research
09 · Step 4 · Literature

Finding papers: the famous research tools

Start here

Google Scholar

The universal starting point. Search papers, see how often each is cited, and follow "Cited by" to newer work. Free.

AI search

Semantic Scholar

A free, AI-powered academic search engine. Understands meaning, not just keywords, and shows short AI summaries of papers.

Ask a question

Elicit

Type your research question — it finds relevant papers and arranges their findings into a comparison table for you.

Evidence check

Consensus

Ask a question like "Does X improve Y?" — it answers using published studies and shows how many agree or disagree.

Explore

ResearchRabbit

Give it one good paper — it maps all the related papers around it, like a "Spotify for research papers". Free.

Visual map

Connected Papers

Draws a visual graph of how papers on your topic connect to each other — great for seeing the key works at a glance.

Trust check

Scite

Shows whether later studies supported or contradicted a paper's claims — not just how many times it was cited.

Good habit

Keep a search log

Note the tool, the keywords, and the date each time you search. Later you can repeat, extend, and defend your search.

Chatbots invent references — these tools find REAL papers. Use the right tool for this job.Real papers come from real databases
10 · Step 5 · Reading

Read and summarise papers faster

NotebookLM (Google, free). Upload your PDFs — papers, notes, reports. Ask questions and it answers only from your files, showing exactly which source and passage it used. It can even create a podcast-style audio discussion of your papers.

ChatPDF & SciSpace. Upload one paper and chat with it: "What was the sample size?", "Which method did they use?", "What are the limitations?"

Why the prompt on the right is safe: asking for page numbers and "Not reported" forces the AI to stay inside the real paper instead of filling gaps with imagination.

Copy this prompt — attach the paper's PDF
I am attaching a research paper.
Summarise it in a table with these rows:

• Aim of the study
• Method and design
• Sample (who and how many)
• Key findings
• Limitations

Rules:
– For every row, mention the page number
  where you found the information.
– If something is not mentioned in the
  paper, write "Not reported".
– Do not add anything from outside
  this paper.
Summaries speed up reading — but for papers central to your work, always read the original.Summaries guide you · sources convince you
11 · Step 6 · Analysis

Analyse your data — with AI as your guide

Describe your data first. Tell the AI your columns, data types and sample size. The more it knows about your data, the better its advice.

Ask "which test?" before "give me code". Choosing the right statistical test is the real decision — the code comes after.

Ask for explanations with every step. AI can teach you SPSS, Python, R or Excel while helping you — demand "explain in simple words" every time.

Never report what you cannot explain. Run everything yourself, check the output makes sense, and be ready to defend every number.

Copy this prompt — describe YOUR data
I have an Excel file of [200 university
students] with these columns:
[age, gender, daily_screen_hours, GPA].

I want to check whether
[daily screen hours] is related to [GPA].

1. Which statistical test is suitable,
   and why — in simple words?
2. Give me step-by-step instructions
   to run it in [SPSS].
3. How do I check the assumptions of
   this test before trusting the result?
4. How should I report the result in
   my thesis?
The same prompt works for Python, R, or Excel — just change the tool's name.Understand first · report second
12 · Step 7 · Writing

Writing with AI: what is fine — and what is not

Fine — and encouraged

Let AI improve the FORM of your writing

Fix grammar and flow — correct mistakes and make sentences smoother (tools like Grammarly and Paperpal specialise in this).
Improve structure — reorganise a chapter, suggest better headings, tighten a long paragraph.
Adapt and translate — turn a chapter into a conference abstract, simplify for a general audience, translate drafts.
Format references — arrange your (real, verified) references into APA or another style.
Not allowed — ever

Never let AI invent the SUBSTANCE

No AI-written thesis — submitting AI text as your own thinking is plagiarism and is easily suspected by examiners.
No unverified references — using a citation you never opened and checked yourself.
No analysis you don't understand — presenting AI-generated results you cannot explain or defend.
No hidden use — most universities and journals now require you to declare significant AI use. Follow their policy.
Simple test: could you defend every sentence, face-to-face, without the AI?AI polishes the words · you own the ideas
13 · Safe use

Five golden rules for using AI in research

1

Verify everything

Every fact, number and reference from AI must be checked against a real source before it enters your work. No exceptions.

2

Protect your data

Do not upload private, sensitive or unpublished data (interviews, patient data, unpublished results) into public AI tools without permission.

3

Disclose honestly

Check your university's and target journal's AI policy, and declare significant AI use the way they require.

4

Stay the author

AI drafts and suggests; you decide. Every idea, claim and conclusion in your work must be one you understand and chose.

5

Keep learning yourself

If your understanding disappears the moment the chat closes, you learned nothing. Use AI to build skill — not to avoid building it.

These rules are what separate an AI-powered researcher from an AI-dependent one.Use the power · keep the judgement

15 · Your turn · Part 2

Practice: find a real paper on your question

Step 1 — Take the research question you just chose and run Prompt B on the right. It gives you ready-made search keywords.

Step 2 — Open Google Scholar (scholar.google.com) or Elicit (elicit.com) and paste one of the search strings.

Step 3 — Pick ONE paper that looks relevant. Note its title, authors and year — this is a real, verifiable source.

Step 4 (bonus) — If the paper's PDF is available, upload it to ChatPDF or NotebookLM and try the summary-table prompt from earlier.

Prompt B — paste your question
My research question is:
[paste your chosen question here]

1. Give me 6 search keywords and phrases
   for finding academic papers on this.
2. Combine them into 2 ready-made search
   strings I can paste into Google Scholar.
3. Which tool would suit this search best —
   Google Scholar, Elicit, or Semantic
   Scholar — and why?
Be ready to share: your question + one real paper you found.AI made the keywords · the database found the paper · you judged it
